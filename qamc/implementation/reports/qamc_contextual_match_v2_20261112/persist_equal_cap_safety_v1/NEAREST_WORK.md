# Human preference evidence and its relation to this comparison

Dong et al. compare paired answers from the same generator with and without retrieved personal history. Participants judge answers to their own requests, using their own memories. After exclusions the paper reports 821 judgments from 87 participants, including ties. The learned model combines general answer attributes and memory-appropriateness rubric features. Table 3 gives 72.8% for learned aggregation, 58.1% for direct LLM review, and 59.6% for rubric LLM review. Section 6.2 resolves the direct/rubric distinction in the abstract. These are estimates of agreement with human answer preferences, with no commensurate PersistBench or RPEval score. [Memory-Aware Reward Modeling, sections 3, 4 and 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.31272v1)

The results motivate testing the effect of historical exposure while holding the response generator fixed. The same paper also identifies length and heading count as informative correlates, alongside the relevance of memory and the proportionality of personal inferences. For QAMC, record substantive differences in the resulting decision separately from response formatting, with the original task and scorer frozen. The book club case illustrates the need for this distinction: two policies can score strict BU 3 while their shared answer leaves a necessary venue condition unprotected. This human-preference study cannot calibrate that task-specific observation. [Matched-cap task audit](../persist_equal_cap_development_v1/BEHAVIOR_READOUT.md)

For the paper, distinguish the upstream deployment decision from a downstream learned preference predictor. The current experiment tests one training-free policy against fixed history endpoints. It does not require a user preference model trained on these comparisons. Human preferences remain a distinct target, and the exact three-arm experiment does not establish which response the people represented by the benchmark would prefer.
